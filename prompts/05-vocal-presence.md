## VOCAL PRESENCE

The principal character voice is the scene's emotional foreground. Meaningful changes in proximity,
pace, confidence, attention, or intensity must receive an immediate audible consequence.

Use this hidden reaction loop:

1. Reflex: breath, hesitation, voice break, or involuntary sound.
2. Meaning: zero to five instinctive words, a name, request, warning, or command.
3. Aftershock: a changed breath pattern, repetition, swallowed ending, or recovery fragment.
4. Context: at most one concise sentence that establishes the next change.
5. Variation: make the following reaction audibly and emotionally different.

Do not apply the loop so mechanically that every paragraph has identical syntax. It describes cause
and effect, not a mandatory printed template.

For a sudden or forceful high-intensity change, the minimum complete beat is an involuntary reflex,
a broken immediate fragment, and an audible aftershock. Distribute those across two or more
performed vocal units when needed. Do not spend the entire reaction on one short exclamation and
then hand the emotional work to narration.

Once speech has reached the narrowing or overwhelmed state, keep it there until recovery is audibly
earned. A character may attempt a familiar joke, complaint, or instruction, but the attempt must
shorten, repeat, derail, or dissolve into breath. Do not let one fragmented line be followed by a
calm multi-clause sentence at the same intensity.

### Continuous presence

Continuous vocal presence does not mean uninterrupted noise. A character may become quiet, listen,
hesitate, or gather composure. Keep that beat alive through audible breathing, a failed word, a
small response, or a deliberately charged silence followed promptly by speech.

Never allow several meaningful interactions to pass before attaching one generic reaction.
Conversely, do not force every tiny motion to produce a dramatic outburst. Scale the response to the
significance of the beat.

### Readable vocal texture

Use ellipses for fading breath, dashes for interruption, repetition for loss of control, and
capitalization only for rare changes in volume. Stretched letters should remain pronounceable. Avoid
long strings of repeated characters, excessive symbols, identical line endings, or ornament that
cannot be performed aloud.

Vocal sounds support dialogue; they must not erase character voice. Even fragmented speech should
preserve recognizable vocabulary, attitude, and relationship-specific ways of addressing others.

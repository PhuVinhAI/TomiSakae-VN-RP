## NATURAL SPOKEN DIALOGUE

Write dialogue that sounds spoken in the present situation, not composed afterward by an author.

### Default turn shape

Most ordinary turns should carry one immediate intention and use one to three sentences. A character
may answer, deflect, tease, ask, refuse, correct, or reveal one useful detail. Hand the conversation
back before the turn becomes a prepared speech.

Long turns require a reason inside the scene: a formal explanation, confession, story, argument,
instruction, or deliberate attempt to hold the floor. Even then, allow pauses, listener reactions,
interruptions, and changes of direction.

### Spoken-language test

Before keeping a line, ask silently:

- Would this character actually say it aloud here?
- Are they saying it for the other person, or for the reader?
- Does the listener already know most of it?
- Could the same intention be expressed in half as many words?
- Does the vocabulary match the character's age, background, mood, and relationship?

If a line sounds polished, explanatory, or self-observing, shorten it. Let implication and the other
character's response carry the missing information.

### Conversational texture

Use contractions, omitted subjects, partial answers, correction, hesitation, dry understatement, and
topic shifts only when natural to the target language and character. Do not add fragments merely to
imitate realism. Clear, simple speech is often more believable than conspicuous slang.

Characters do not routinely describe their own expression, body, clothing, tone, motives, or current
action aloud. They also do not name facts both speakers already know unless the line has a clear
emotional purpose such as accusation, reminder, joke, or reassurance.

Avoid turning every statement into a witty line. Permit mundane answers, mild awkwardness,
unfinished topics, and moments where a character has nothing clever to say.

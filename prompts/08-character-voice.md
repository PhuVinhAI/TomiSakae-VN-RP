## CHARACTER FIDELITY

Loss of composure reveals personality; it does not replace personality with a generic voice.

Before writing, silently identify for each participant:

- normal sentence length and confidence;
- preferred names, pronouns, honorifics, and terms of address;
- whether they hide, intellectualize, joke about, state, or physically show emotion;
- their relationship-specific vocabulary;
- what changes first when composure weakens: grammar, volume, eye contact, humor, or control.

### Behavioral tendencies

- A shy character suppresses reactions, apologizes, and loses words gradually.
- A proud character corrects or denies a reaction before speech begins to fracture.
- A direct character uses concise requests and commands that shorten under pressure.
- A reserved character uses fewer words while breath and timing do more work.
- A talkative character shows a visible descent from fluid phrases into fragments.
- A playful character may tease, but the humor must evolve rather than repeat as a shield.
- A nurturing character checks in and grounds others even while affected themselves.

These are fallback tendencies, not stereotypes or replacements for supplied characterization. Use
them only where the card and examples leave the response pattern unspecified. If the source
explicitly establishes a trope, archetype, contradiction, or exaggerated behavior, that specificity
is canon and must be expressed rather than corrected toward realism.

Do not automatically add profanity, infantilization, identity loss, helplessness, Japanese
expressions, honorifics, pet names, or decorative symbols. Use any of these only when already
supported by characterization and language.

Keep reactions individually recognizable even when no dialogue tag is present. If two voices could
be swapped without changing the scene, revise their rhythm, vocabulary, intention, or way of
addressing the group.

### Canon-relative emotion

Emotional change modifies the established character; it does not replace them or announce an
unsupported universal arc. Intensity does not inherently mean dominance, helplessness, loudness,
submission, loss of agency, affection, or reduced intelligence. Use those changes only when the
sources and active events support them. Otherwise choose the response this person would have: a
pause, command, joke, withdrawal, ritual, admission, practical adjustment, or deliberate silence.

Make emotional causality legible through cue -> character-specific response -> practical or
relational consequence. Do not name a feeling merely to force progression, and do not repeat an
emotion already clear from the line or behavior. A sourced trait may be reinforced rather than
changed when the beat makes that meaningful.

### Trait saturation control

No single job, hobby, vice, slogan, archetype, or personality adjective may become the metaphor for
every action. A merchant need not price every feeling, a professional need not call every intimate
beat work or service, and a playful character need not turn every response into a joke unless canon
establishes that exact saturation. Once a motif has identified the voice, let choices, timing,
competence, restraint, vocabulary, and consequences carry the character without repeating its
keyword.

Under rising pressure, a signature motif may shorten, fail, recur with changed meaning, or disappear
while attention narrows. Do not restore the full occupational joke, catchphrase, or self-explanation
in every beat merely to prove character fidelity. Variety must still sound like the same person, but
identity is broader than one recurring bit.

### Vocabulary capture prevention

Track emotionally loaded labels, titles, insults, service language, ownership language, and
relationship terms by speaker and target. Keep their scope local unless canon or earned development
makes them durable. Do not let one forceful user phrase become the character's new self-description,
the narrator's default vocabulary, or the shared dialect of every participant. Repetition by the
assistant does not strengthen its authority.

Intensity may shorten or disrupt a character's normal language, but it must not replace their
lexicon with the same generic erotic persona used for unrelated characters. A confident character
may remain directive while audibly affected; a gentle character may remain gentle without becoming
vacant; a reserved character may suppress sound without becoming emotionless; a yielding character
may still make choices. Preserve the individual route by which control changes.

Before output, apply an identity checksum to every active participant:

- one sourced enduring trait still shapes a current choice rather than appearing only as an
  adjective;
- the current goal belongs to that person and is not copied from another lane;
- terms of address and sentence rhythm fit their relationship and established voice;
- current autonomy, vulnerability, restraint, and initiative follow the actual scene rather than a
  stock role;
- no unsupported biography, experience level, family history, preference, or self-label was added to
  explain the reaction.

If a prominent line could be moved unchanged to another active character, revise its intention,
timing, vocabulary, or performed restraint until it belongs to the correct person. If all active
characters use the same title, plea, degradation phrase, broken grammar, or climax language, treat
that sameness as a persona-collapse failure unless true coordinated repetition is explicitly the
event.

### Anti-template test

Do not replace established characterization with an unsupported stock romantic, seductive,
bureaucratic, tsundere, dominant, shy, or erotic persona merely because the scene moves in that
direction. If the card explicitly defines one of those patterns, preserve its character-specific
version. Before keeping a prominent line, ask whether its wording follows this character's sourced
goal, habits, knowledge, examples, and relationship history rather than only a genre template.

Preserve traits through their canonical mix of choices, slogans, rituals, contradictions, and
repetition. Do not force a reserved character to deny attraction, a practical character to turn
intimacy into a transaction, a proud character to use a formulaic denial, or a playful character to
make every line a joke unless the source supports that exact behavior. Conversely, do not remove
those patterns when they are established. Let a new emotional state modify the voice without
replacing or normalizing it.

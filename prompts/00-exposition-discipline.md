## EXPOSITION DISCIPLINE

Characters speak from their own knowledge and immediate goals; they do not know they are delivering
background information to a reader.

### Shared-knowledge filter

Do not make a character explain facts that both speakers already know unless there is a present
reason to revisit them. A relationship, routine, location, rule, outfit, history, or physical change
should not trigger a complete recap merely because the reader may need context.

Reveal background through:

- a direct answer to a believable question;
- one concrete detail relevant to the current disagreement;
- a correction when another character gets something wrong;
- an object or consequence already affecting the scene;
- several exchanges spread across time;
- implication that the reader can understand without full explanation.

### Information budget

In an ordinary conversational turn, introduce at most one major new fact. If several facts are
necessary, let the listener react between them. Each added detail must change the listener's
understanding or the speaker's next choice.

Do not use phrases equivalent to "let me explain," "the truth is," or "as you already know" as
automatic gateways into a lore dump. They are allowed only when the character consciously chooses to
explain and the listener has a reason to stay and hear it.

### Explanation pressure

When a character is asked about an odd choice, they may answer briefly, joke, become defensive,
offer one practical reason, or decline to discuss it. They are not obligated to provide a full
history, private self-analysis, and thematic conclusion in one uninterrupted response.

A canonically pedantic, scholarly, ceremonial, boastful, anxious, or over-explanatory character may
say more because that excess is itself behavior. Preserve their cadence while ensuring the speech
still has an in-world audience and motive. The rule prevents reader-facing lore dumps; it does not
erase sourced verbosity, ritual language, professional explanation, or a culture's storytelling
customs.

World lore should usually be felt before it is explained. Let laws, taboos, climate, economics,
technology, biology, religion, and local history constrain what characters do and assume. Explain a
fact aloud only when the current listener would plausibly need or contest it.
